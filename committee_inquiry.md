provides a nationally consistent, science based understanding of climate risks across Australia's systems, sectors and communities.

To deliver this, the Australian Climate Service drew on the expertise of more than 250 scientists and specialists from across government, academia and the private sector, bringing together a breadth of information that can meaningfully inform climate adaptation. The methodology aligns with international best practice, including guidance from the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change. It integrates hazard exposure, vulnerability and response, ensuring the assessment is robust, credible and tailored to Australia's decision-making needs. The national assessment provides a point-in-time snapshot of climate risks under three global warming scenarios: 1.5 degrees, two degrees and three degrees. It shows what is at risk, where and how.

If current levels of adaptation remain unchanged, risks across all systems are projected to increase significantly by 2050. This work shifts the national focus from a reactive disaster response to proactive risk management. CHAIR:  Dr Wylie, while we have you and we're talking about the health impacts of these climate disasters and this climate risk, I'm interested in your medical opinion as a doctor in South Australia on the recent health advice from the South Australian government in relation to the toxic algae bloom. This health advice wasn't publicised properly to people until after our Senate inquiry last week. But I'm interested as to what, as a doctor, your advice is to people who have asthma or respiratory issues. Should they be anywhere near the bloom?

Dr Wylie:  No, they should keep away. I first became aware of the bloom in April, I think it was—March or April—and then we had people, anecdotally, talking about local skin irritation if they go down to the beach and people having increased shortness of breath. It only stands to reason that, if you already have an underlying respiratory condition and you put another assault into your system, it's going to be more and more of a problem.

I do understand where SA Health was coming from, because of the lack of evidence. Unfortunately, this is what's happening with climate change. We're in a position where our normal way of doing medicine is that you study a system, you gather your evidence, you undergo robust testing and then you can say, 'Okay, this is what we know,' but, because climate change impacts are changing so hard and fast, we don't have that luxury of time anymore. Sometimes we just have to apply a little bit of common sense and say, 'If you know you've got shortness of breath because you're walking down the beach, don't go there, or avoid it.' I've been talking to my patients with asthma and I'm saying, 'Avoid it if you can,' and to make sure they've got their preventers on board and that their asthma is as well managed as it possibly can be. Of course, there are the great mental health impacts that we're seeing in our communities. For anybody going down to the beach, it's not hard to see ecological collapse on your beaches, and you see all the dead, rotting carcasses everywhere. It's very, very confronting. So the mental health impacts of climate change are absolutely writ large in the South Australian community at the moment.

CHAIR:  I'm also interested in the comments from state officials that if you go down to the beach, if you're impacted by the toxic algae—and now we know there are brevetoxins in the water, at least along the metro beaches—you might have cold or flu symptoms for a few days.
